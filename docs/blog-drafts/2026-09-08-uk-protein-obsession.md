### AI Summary (for the "AI Summary" button, not part of the body)

Protein has become the UK's favourite marketing word, with protein bars,
yoghurts and even water flooding supermarket shelves as the market heads
toward roughly $1.4 billion by the mid 2030s. But most UK adults already eat
more than the official Reference Nutrient Intake of 0.75g per kilogram of
bodyweight, around 45 to 55g a day for most people. A "high protein" claim is
a strict legal threshold (at least 20% of a food's energy from protein), not
a health guarantee: a November 2025 investigation found 37% of "healthy"
snack bars sold in the UK are high in sugar and 55% are high in saturated
fat, protein claim or not. The people who genuinely benefit from extra
protein are older adults protecting muscle mass, strength and endurance
athletes, pregnant and breastfeeding women, and anyone recovering from
illness or surgery. For everyone else, the smartest move is to check the
protein-to-sugar ratio and ingredient list rather than trust the front of
the pack, and to remember that eggs, yoghurt, beans and chicken deliver
protein without any of the marketing.

## BODY (paste exactly as-is into the post body field)

<p>Walk down any UK supermarket aisle right now and it feels like every product has been rebranded overnight. Yoghurt is protein yoghurt. Water has protein added to it. Even ready meals boast "30g protein per portion" in letters bigger than the name of the actual dish. Protein has become the nutrient everyone is chasing, and food brands know it.</p>

<p>But how much protein do you actually need? And when a label shouts "high protein," does that automatically make the product a good choice? The answers are more interesting, and more useful, than the marketing suggests.</p>

<h2>Why everyone's suddenly obsessed with protein</h2>

<p>Protein has quietly become the most valuable word on a UK food label. Gym culture, weight loss trends built around feeling fuller for longer, and a steady stream of fitness content online have all pushed the same message: eat more protein. Supermarkets and manufacturers have responded by slapping a protein claim on everything from crisps to chocolate mousse.</p>

<div class="article-stat">
  <p class="article-stat-lead"><span class="article-stat-num">$551.6m</span> is the size of the UK protein snacks market in 2026 alone</p>
  <p class="article-stat-pill">and it is forecast to reach <strong>$1.4 billion</strong> within a decade</p>
</div>

<p>That kind of growth does not happen because shoppers suddenly became malnourished. It happens because "high protein" has become shorthand for "healthy," and shorthand sells. The trouble is that shorthand skips two important questions: how much protein your body genuinely needs, and what a food actually has to contain to legally use that claim.</p>

<h2>How much protein do you actually need</h2>

<p>The NHS Reference Nutrient Intake (RNI) for protein is 0.75g per kilogram of bodyweight per day for the average adult. It sounds clinical, but in practice it works out lower than most people expect.</p>

  <p class="article-numbers-subtitle">NHS Reference Nutrient Intake</p>
  <h3 class="article-numbers-title">What "enough" protein actually looks like</h3>
  <p class="article-numbers-text">Based on 0.75g of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day.</p>
  <div class="article-numbers-grid">
    <div class="article-numbers-item">
      <span class="article-numbers-stat">45g</span>
      <p class="article-numbers-item-text">a day for an average 60kg woman</p>
    </div>
    <div class="article-numbers-item">
      <span class="article-numbers-stat">55g</span>
      <p class="article-numbers-item-text">a day for an average 75kg man</p>
    </div>
    <div class="article-numbers-item">
      <span class="article-numbers-stat">50g</span>
      <p class="article-numbers-item-text">the standard reference intake used on food labels</p>
    </div>
    <div class="article-numbers-item">
      <span class="article-numbers-stat">76g</span>
      <p class="article-numbers-item-text">the average actual daily intake for UK adults aged 19 to 64</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<p>Look closely at those last two figures. The average UK adult already eats around 50% more protein than the minimum requirement, without a single protein bar or shake. Even adults over 65, whose average intake drops to 67g a day, are still comfortably above the RNI. For the overwhelming majority of people eating a normal, varied UK diet, a protein deficiency simply is not the problem it is being marketed as.</p>

<blockquote>The question was never really whether you need more protein. It is whether the snack in your hand is actually a smart way to get it.</blockquote>

<h2>What "high protein" actually means on a label</h2>

<p>Here is something most shoppers do not realise: "high protein" is not a marketing phrase a brand gets to interpret however it likes. It is a legally defined claim under retained EU food law (Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006), and there is a hard threshold behind it.</p>

<div class="article-cardgrid">
  <div class="article-cardgrid-card">
    <div class="article-cardgrid-head">
      <p class="article-cardgrid-label">Lower bar</p>
      <h4 class="article-cardgrid-title">"Source of protein"</h4>
    </div>
    <p class="article-cardgrid-text">At least 12% of the food's total energy must come from protein.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="article-cardgrid-card">
    <div class="article-cardgrid-head">
      <p class="article-cardgrid-label">Higher bar</p>
      <h4 class="article-cardgrid-title">"High protein"</h4>
    </div>
    <p class="article-cardgrid-text">At least 20% of the food's total energy must come from protein. Protein provides about 4 kcal per gram, so the maths has to actually add up before the claim is legal.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<p>That threshold is a nutrition rule, not a health rule. It says nothing about the sugar, saturated fat, or salt sitting alongside that protein in the same product. A food can comfortably clear the 20% bar and still be, by any sensible definition, a treat rather than a health food.</p>

<h2>The catch: high protein does not mean healthy</h2>

<p>This is where a lot of otherwise sensible shopping decisions go wrong. A November 2025 investigation by Action on Salt & Sugar at Queen Mary University of London looked at "healthy" snack bars sold across major UK supermarkets, including many carrying high-protein or high-fibre claims.</p>

<div class="article-stat">
  <p class="article-stat-lead"><span class="article-stat-num">37%</span> of "healthy" snack bars sold in the UK are high in sugar</p>
  <p class="article-stat-pill">and <strong>55%</strong> are high in saturated fat, under the UK's own traffic light labelling system</p>
</div>

<p>None of this means protein snacks are a scam. It means the protein claim is only ever one line on the label, not the whole story. A genuinely good high-protein snack and a chocolate bar wearing a protein badge can sit right next to each other on the same shelf, and only the small print tells them apart.</p>

<h2>Who actually needs more protein than an average diet provides</h2>

<p>There are real groups of people who benefit from paying closer attention to protein, and it is worth knowing if you are one of them.</p>

<div class="article-cardgrid">
  <div class="article-cardgrid-card">
    <div class="article-cardgrid-head">
      <h4 class="article-cardgrid-title">Older adults</h4>
    </div>
    <p class="article-cardgrid-text">Muscle mass naturally declines with age, a process called sarcopenia. Slightly higher protein intake, spread across meals, helps protect strength and mobility.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="article-cardgrid-card">
    <div class="article-cardgrid-head">
      <h4 class="article-cardgrid-title">Strength and endurance athletes</h4>
    </div>
    <p class="article-cardgrid-text">Regular resistance training or heavy endurance training increases the amount of protein needed to repair and build muscle tissue.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="article-cardgrid-card">
    <div class="article-cardgrid-head">
      <h4 class="article-cardgrid-title">Pregnant and breastfeeding women</h4>
    </div>
    <p class="article-cardgrid-text">Protein needs rise to support the growth of the baby and, later, milk production.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="article-cardgrid-card">
    <div class="article-cardgrid-head">
      <h4 class="article-cardgrid-title">Anyone recovering from illness or surgery</h4>
    </div>
    <p class="article-cardgrid-text">The body needs extra protein to repair tissue and rebuild strength during recovery, often at a time when appetite is lowest.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<p>If none of those apply to you, that is not a reason to ignore protein altogether. It just means the goal is choosing good sources within a normal diet, not chasing a number that is already comfortably met.</p>

<h2>How to actually spot a good high-protein snack</h2>

<p>Rather than trusting the front of the pack, a few quick habits do a much better job of separating a genuinely useful high-protein product from a well-marketed treat.</p>

<div class="article-numlist">
  <div class="article-numlist-item"><div class="article-numlist-content">
    <p class="article-numlist-title">Check the protein-to-sugar ratio, not just the protein number</p>
    <p class="article-numlist-desc">A bar with 15g of protein and 15g of sugar is a very different product to one with 15g of protein and 3g of sugar.</p>
  </div></div>
  <div class="article-numlist-item"><div class="article-numlist-content">
    <p class="article-numlist-title">Look at the traffic light colours, not just the claim</p>
    <p class="article-numlist-desc">A "high protein" front label can still sit above an amber or red traffic light for sugar or saturated fat on the back. The back of the pack tells the real story.</p>
  </div></div>
  <div class="article-numlist-item"><div class="article-numlist-content">
    <p class="article-numlist-title">Scan the ingredient list, not just the nutrition panel</p>
    <p class="article-numlist-desc">Ingredients are listed by weight. If sugar, syrup or chocolate coating appears before the protein source, the name on the front is doing some heavy lifting.</p>
  </div></div>
  <div class="article-numlist-item"><div class="article-numlist-content">
    <p class="article-numlist-title">Remember whole foods count as protein too</p>
    <p class="article-numlist-desc">Eggs, plain yoghurt, chicken, fish, beans, lentils and tofu all deliver protein without needing a claim on the packet at all.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2>A quick note on plant based protein</h2>

<p>Plant based protein snacks are one of the fastest growing parts of this market in the UK, already accounting for close to half of new protein snack products. Beans, lentils, tofu, tempeh, nuts and seeds all provide protein, and combining a few different plant sources across the day, rather than relying on a single food, is the easiest way to cover the full range of amino acids the body needs. It does not require careful pairing at every single meal, just variety across the week.</p>

    <div class="article-accordion-body"><p>That figure is a popular gym rule of thumb aimed at people doing serious strength training, not an official recommendation for the general population. It sits well above the NHS Reference Nutrient Intake and is not necessary for most people.</p></div>
