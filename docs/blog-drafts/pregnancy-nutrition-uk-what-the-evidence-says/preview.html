Pregnancy Nutrition in the UK: What the Evidence Actually Says

From folic acid to fish limits, pregnancy nutrition advice can feel like a minefield of myths and conflicting rules. Here is what the NHS and the evidence actually say about eating well in pregnancy, plus a real change coming to UK bread this winter.

Pregnancy comes with more food advice than anyone could reasonably keep track of. Your auntie has opinions. A stranger in the supermarket queue has opinions. Half of it contradicts what your midwife actually told you, and it is no wonder so many people either panic over a slice of brie they ate before they knew the rules, or give up trying to follow any of it because it all feels like too much.

The good news is that UK pregnancy nutrition advice is actually fairly short once you strip away the myths. It comes down to a handful of nutrients worth prioritising, a short list of genuine food safety risks, and a couple of habits worth pausing for a few months. There is also a real change happening to UK bread this winter that is worth knowing about, whether you are pregnant right now or just thinking ahead.

Here is what actually matters, based on NHS guidance, advice from the UK's Chief Medical Officers, and the Food Standards Agency, and what you can safely stop worrying about.

Forget "eating for two"

This is probably the most persistent myth in pregnancy nutrition, and it is simply not true. You do not need to double your portions, and you do not need extra calories at all for most of the pregnancy. The NHS guidance is that additional energy needs only appear in the third trimester, and even then it is a modest extra amount, roughly the equivalent of a slice of wholemeal toast with a scraping of butter, not a second dinner.

What matters far more than quantity is quality. A handful of nutrients become genuinely more important during pregnancy, and most people in the UK are not getting quite enough of them even before they conceive.

The nutrients actually worth paying attention to

Rather than trying to overhaul your entire diet, it is far more useful to focus on getting a small number of things right.

Folic acid

400 micrograms a day

Take a supplement from when you start trying to conceive until at least the end of week 12. It helps prevent neural tube defects such as spina bifida, and the neural tube forms in the very earliest weeks, often before a pregnancy is even confirmed.

Vitamin D

10 micrograms a day

Recommended throughout pregnancy and breastfeeding, since UK sunlight is not reliable enough to guarantee levels on its own, especially outside the summer months.

Iron

Blood volume increases significantly in pregnancy, and iron needs rise with it. Red meat, pulses, dark leafy greens and fortified cereals all help, paired with a source of vitamin C to improve absorption.

Omega 3

Oily fish supports the baby's brain and eye development. Aim for up to two portions a week of salmon, mackerel, sardines or trout, balancing the benefit against mercury limits below.

400mcg of folic acid a day is what the NHS recommends before conception and through the first trimester

Yet many people do not start taking it until after a positive test, by which point the neural tube may have already begun forming

The big change landing in UK bread this winter

From December 2026, it becomes a legal requirement for all non wholemeal wheat flour sold in the UK to be fortified with folic acid, with some products already carrying it from autumn 2025 as manufacturers phase it in early. This is a genuinely significant public health change, and it has been decades in the making. Countries including the United States, Canada and Australia have fortified flour for years, and the UK is only now catching up.

The expected impact is a reduction in neural tube defects of around 20 percent nationally, simply from folic acid reaching people through everyday bread, pasta and baked goods, including those who were never planning a pregnancy or did not know they had conceived yet.

Fortification is designed to support, not replace, the existing advice to take a folic acid supplement before and during early pregnancy.

That last point matters. The amount added to flour is not enough on its own to reach the recommended intake, and it will not appear in wholemeal flour, gluten free products, or non wheat flours like spelt or soya. If you are pregnant or trying to conceive, you still need the supplement. Think of the fortified flour as a safety net for the wider population, not a replacement for your own routine.

The foods actually worth avoiding, and why

This is the part that generates the most anxiety, largely because a lot of the advice circulating online is either outdated or wildly overcautious. The real list is shorter than you would think, and every item on it is there for a specific, evidence based reason.

A few clarifications that tend to get lost in translation. Lion stamped eggs, which cover the vast majority of UK hen eggs, are considered safe to eat raw or runny because the hens are vaccinated against salmonella. Tuna is not banned, just capped, at around two tuna steaks or four medium tins a week, because it carries more mercury than other oily fish without quite reaching the level of swordfish or shark. And soft cheeses are only a problem raw. Bake a wedge of camembert and it is perfectly fine.

What about toxoplasmosis specifically

  • Wash fruit, vegetables and salad thoroughly, including anything labelled ready to eat
  • Wear gloves when gardening or handling soil, and wash your hands afterwards
  • Avoid handling cat litter where possible, or wear gloves and wash your hands immediately if you have to
  • Cook meat all the way through, with no pink left in the middle

Drinks: caffeine, alcohol and what is actually fine

Caffeine crosses the placenta, and a developing baby cannot process it the way an adult can, which is why the advice is to cap it rather than cut it entirely. The NHS recommends staying under 200mg a day, which is roughly the amount below.

That means a single mug of filter coffee already uses most of your daily allowance, but a cup of tea and a small bar of chocolate later in the day still leaves room to spare. Decaf is not entirely caffeine free either, though the amount is small enough that it rarely matters unless you are drinking it by the pot.

Alcohol is more straightforward. There is no known safe amount to drink in pregnancy, and no safe time to drink it, so the advice from the UK Chief Medical Officers is to avoid it completely. This is not about degree, since even small, occasional amounts have not been shown to be risk free, which is why the guidance does not attempt to set a safe limit the way it does for caffeine.

Eating well when someone else is doing the cooking

Restaurants, takeaways and ready meals are where most of the practical questions come up, since you cannot see what is happening in someone else's kitchen. A few habits go a long way here.

Check ready meals are piping hot all the way through

Stir halfway through microwaving and let it stand, since microwaves can heat unevenly and leave cold spots.

Be cautious with buffets and food that has been sitting out

Bacteria multiply fastest at room temperature, so freshly served hot food is a safer bet than something that has been under a heat lamp for a while.

Ask if you are unsure rather than guessing

Staff at most restaurants and cafes are used to being asked whether a dish contains pate, unpasteurised cheese or raw egg, particularly for things like mousse, mayonnaise or fresh pasta sauces.

Common questions

Can I eat sushi while pregnant?

Yes, in most cases. UK food safety rules require fish intended to be eaten raw to be frozen first, which kills parasites. Most UK supermarkets and sushi restaurants follow this. The usual pregnancy limits on oily fish and mercury still apply if the sushi contains salmon or tuna.

Is soft serve ice cream safe?

Yes. Soft serve machines use a pasteurised mix, so the ice cream itself is not the concern. Good hygiene and regular cleaning of the machine matter more than the product, and that is true for everyone, not just pregnant customers.

Do I need to avoid peanuts?

No. Current UK guidance no longer recommends avoiding peanuts during pregnancy unless you are allergic yourself. Earlier advice to avoid them has since been reversed after further research found no link to a higher risk of allergy in the baby.

What about herbal and fruit teas?

Most are fine in normal amounts, though it is worth keeping variety rather than drinking large quantities of a single type, since the safety evidence for some herbal ingredients in pregnancy is limited. Peppermint and raspberry leaf tea are generally considered fine, but check with your midwife if you are drinking them regularly, particularly raspberry leaf earlier in pregnancy.

Can I still have a takeaway curry or pizza?

Yes. There is nothing inherently unsafe about a normal takeaway, as long as it is cooked and served hot. The same caution around raw egg mayonnaise, unpasteurised cheese toppings or undercooked meat applies as it would anywhere else.

The Bottom Line

Pregnancy nutrition in the UK is mostly about addition, not restriction. Get the folic acid and vitamin D right, eat a reasonably varied diet, and keep a short mental list of things to avoid or limit for a few months. Everything else is far more flexible than the internet tends to make it sound.
