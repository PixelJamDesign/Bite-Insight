Turn over almost any packet in a UK supermarket and somewhere in the ingredients list you will spot a code that looks like it belongs on a lab report rather than a dinner plate. E102. E407. E621. Most of us scroll straight past them, and fair enough, they are not exactly inviting. But those codes are not secret or sinister. They are a labelling system, and once you know how to read it, a label full of E numbers stops looking like a warning and starts looking like information.

What an E number actually is

An E number is simply a code given to a food additive once it has been assessed and approved for use in the UK and the EU. The "E" stands for Europe, a leftover from when the system was set up to create one shared approvals list across the continent. Britain kept the same numbering after leaving the EU, so the codes on a UK label still mean the same thing they always did.

Getting a code is not a rubber stamp. Before an additive is approved, the Food Standards Agency and its Scottish counterpart review the safety evidence, including how much of the substance a person could realistically eat in a day, and set an acceptable daily intake with a wide safety margin built in. If a manufacturer wants to use a new additive, or use an old one in a new way, it has to go through that process first.

What an E number does not tell you is whether something is natural or artificial. Ascorbic acid, which is simply vitamin C, is E300. Beta carotene, the pigment that makes carrots orange, is E160a. Plenty of E numbers are just familiar substances written in code, which is exactly why learning the system is more useful than fearing it.

The code, decoded

The number itself is not random. Additives are grouped by what they actually do to the food, and the first digit gives the game away.

E100s

Colours

Make food look more appetising or replace colour lost in processing.

E200s

Preservatives

Stop mould, bacteria and yeast from spoiling food too quickly.

E300s

Antioxidants

Stop fats and oils turning rancid and fruit going brown.

E400s

Thickeners and emulsifiers

Give food body and stop oil and water splitting apart.

E500s

Acidity regulators

Control pH so a product tastes and keeps consistently.

E600s

Flavour enhancers

Boost existing taste, like the well known E621, monosodium glutamate.

E900s

Sweeteners and others

Everything from low calorie sweeteners to glazing agents and gases.

Once you can place a code in one of these families, the rest of the label makes a lot more sense. A drink loaded with E100s and E400s is telling you it is chasing colour and mouthfeel. A tinned product full of E200s is telling you it is built to sit on a shelf for a long time.

The Southampton Six: when the evidence actually changed the rules

Not every E number worry is overblown. In 2007, researchers at the University of Southampton, funded by the Food Standards Agency itself, ran a trial on more than 250 children. They gave the children drinks containing mixes of six artificial colours plus the preservative sodium benzoate, and compared their behaviour against a placebo group.

The trial found a measurable increase in hyperactive behaviour in children given the additive mixes

The effect showed up in children with no ADHD diagnosis, not just those already prone to hyperactivity

The researchers could not pin the effect on any single colour, only on the combination, but the result was strong enough that the FSA asked UK manufacturers to voluntarily remove these six colours from food and drink by 2009. The colours in question, often called the Southampton Six, are:

  • E102 (tartrazine)
  • E104 (quinoline yellow)
  • E110 (sunset yellow)
  • E122 (carmoisine)
  • E124 (ponceau 4R)
  • E129 (allura red)

They were never banned outright. If a product still contains any of them, UK and EU rules require the label to carry a specific warning that the colour "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children." That warning line is one of the few places on a label where a single additive gets called out by name, which makes it worth actually reading rather than skipping past.

Sweeteners and the aspartame headline everyone half remembers

You have probably heard some version of "aspartame causes cancer." It is one of the most misquoted food safety stories of the last few years, so it is worth untangling properly.

In 2023, the International Agency for Research on Cancer, part of the World Health Organization, classified aspartame as "possibly carcinogenic to humans." That sounds alarming until you know what the classification actually measures. It is a statement about the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk. Processed meat sits in a higher risk category than aspartame, and so does drinking very hot beverages, yet nobody rewrote the rules on a cup of tea.

On the same day it issued that classification, the WHO's own food safety committee reviewed all the available evidence and left the acceptable daily intake of aspartame completely unchanged.

In practice, that acceptable daily intake works out to roughly the amount in nine to fourteen cans of diet soft drink a day for an average adult, every day, for life, before you would even approach the limit used to set the guidance. That is not a loophole, it is how food safety limits are deliberately built, with a large buffer between the level regulators watch and the level anyone actually consumes.

None of that means every sweetener deserves a free pass, or that you should ignore how your own body responds to them. It does mean the aspartame headline is a good example of why the E number itself, E951 in this case, is rarely the full story. The dose, the source and the regulatory context all matter more than the code alone.

Titanium dioxide: the additive Britain and Europe disagree about

Few additives show how much judgement calls, not just raw data, shape food rules better than titanium dioxide, E171. It is a whitening agent used to make products look brighter and more uniform, from icing to chewing gum to some sauces.

European Union

Banned in food since 2022

EU regulators concluded they could not rule out genotoxicity concerns and withdrew approval for food use across the bloc.

United Kingdom

Still permitted

The FSA and Food Standards Scotland reviewed the same underlying science and concluded there was no safety case for a ban, so E171 remains approved here.

Two food safety bodies, looking at broadly the same body of research, landed in different places. That is a genuinely useful thing to understand about additive regulation generally. It is not always a clean case of dangerous versus safe. Sometimes it is a judgement call about how much uncertainty a regulator is willing to accept, and different regulators draw that line differently. If you want to avoid E171 specifically, checking the ingredients list is currently the only reliable way to do it in the UK, since there is no blanket ban to rely on the way there now is across the EU.

Emulsifiers: the additive you are eating more of than you think

If colours and sweeteners get most of the attention, emulsifiers are the additive family quietly doing the most work in the average UK trolley. A review of UK supermarket products found emulsifiers in just over half of everything checked, and among products that used them, most contained more than one at once.

Emulsifiers are not new or exotic. Lecithin, the most common one by far, occurs naturally in egg yolks and soya beans. What has changed is scale. Ultra processed food relies on emulsifiers to hold together textures that would otherwise separate, so as more of the average UK diet shifts toward ultra processed products, emulsifier exposure rises with it, quietly, one ingredient list at a time. Researchers are still working out whether regularly eating multiple emulsifiers together has effects beyond each one on its own, which makes this one of the more genuinely open questions in food additive science right now.

How to actually read a label without losing your mind

Check the order, not just the list

Ingredients are listed by weight, heaviest first. An additive near the end of a long list is a small fraction of the product.

Learn the six colours, not all two hundred codes

Memorising every E number is not realistic or necessary. Knowing the Southampton Six by sight covers the one group with a specific behavioural warning attached.

Count the number of E numbers, not just their names

A short ingredients list with one or two additives and a long list with a dozen are different products, even if individual additives overlap.

Use a scanning app for the ones you cannot place

If a code does not ring a bell, scanning the barcode is faster and more reliable than guessing or searching a forum post.

Common questions

Are E numbers only used in unhealthy food?

No. Vitamin C, citric acid and beta carotene all carry E numbers and appear in everyday foods that most people would consider healthy. The presence of an E number on its own says nothing about how nutritious a product is.

Does a long ingredients list always mean a worse product?

Not necessarily, but it is a reasonable starting signal. A longer list, especially one with several additives from the same family, usually points to a more heavily processed product than a shorter one made mostly of whole ingredients.

Why do some additives have different rules in the UK and the EU?

Since leaving the EU, the UK runs its own food safety assessments through the Food Standards Agency and Food Standards Scotland. Most of the time the conclusions match the EU's, but occasionally, as with titanium dioxide, the two reach different judgements from a similar evidence base.

Is it worth avoiding additives entirely?

For most people, no single additive at the levels found in food needs to be avoided outright. It makes more sense to pay attention to overall patterns, such as how processed your regular meals are, rather than treating every E number as a red flag on its own.

The Bottom Line

An E number is a code for something approved and assessed, not a verdict. Learn the handful that genuinely warrant attention, such as the Southampton Six colours, and let the rest of the label tell you what it actually is: information you can use, not a threat to decode.
