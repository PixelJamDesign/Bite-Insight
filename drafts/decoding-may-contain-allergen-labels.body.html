Pick up almost any packet in a UK supermarket and somewhere on the back you will find the words "may contain". Nuts, milk, gluten, sesame, it does not seem to matter what the product actually is, that little warning turns up again and again. For someone managing a food allergy, or shopping for a family member who has one, that sentence can be the difference between a product going in the trolley or going straight back on the shelf.

The trouble is that most shoppers have never really been told what "may contain" means, how a business decides to use it, or how it is different from the proper ingredients list a few lines above it. This guide breaks down what a UK food label is legally required to say, what is left to a business's own judgement, and how to read the whole thing with a bit more confidence next time you are standing in the aisle.

Why almost every product seems to carry a warning

The warning you see so often is called precautionary allergen labelling, usually shortened to PAL. It is easy to assume PAL is just part of the ingredients list, but it works completely differently and it is worth understanding that distinction before anything else.

1 in 4 British adults say they live with a food allergy or intolerance

Yet only around 6% of UK adults have a food allergy that has been clinically confirmed, roughly 2.4 million people

An ingredients list has to be accurate. Everything on it was put into the product on purpose, in a known quantity. "May contain" is something else entirely. It is a business's own judgement call about the chance of an allergen getting into a product by accident, through shared equipment, shared production lines, or ingredients stored near each other in the same factory. It is not about anything deliberately added, and it is not required by law in the way a full ingredients list is.

The Food Standards Agency's own guidance says PAL should only be used once a business has actually carried out a risk assessment and found that cross contamination genuinely cannot be controlled through cleaning schedules, timing, or keeping ingredients physically apart on the production line. When a warning is used, the FSA recommends it names the specific allergen, "may contain peanuts" rather than a vague "may contain nuts", so shoppers know exactly what they are weighing up.

In practice, that recommendation is not always followed. Getting the assessment wrong and leaving a warning off a product carries real legal and reputational risk for a manufacturer, so it is tempting to add "may contain" across an entire product range as a blanket safety net, whether or not the risk is significant for that specific item. The FSA has flagged this directly, warning that overusing the label narrows consumer choice and can push people with allergies away from foods that were perfectly safe for them all along.

The 14 allergens UK law actually requires

Separate from PAL, there is a hard legal requirement. Every prepacked food sold in the UK has to declare any of 14 major allergens if they are used as an ingredient, however small the amount. This is not a judgement call. If an allergen is in the product on purpose, it has to be named, full stop.

Since October 2021, that requirement has also covered food that is prepared and packed on site before being sold, such as a sandwich made in a shop that morning. This change is known as Natasha's Law, named after Natasha Ednan-Laperouse, a teenager who died after eating a Pret a Manger baguette that contained sesame not listed on the packaging. On a label, all 14 allergens have to stand out from the rest of the ingredients, usually shown in bold. Here is the full list, along with some of the everyday places each one tends to turn up.

"May contain" versus the ingredients list, what is actually different

Once you know PAL and the ingredients list are two different systems, the label starts to make a lot more sense. One tells you what is definitely in the product. The other tells you what might be in it by accident. Here is the difference side by side.

Full ingredients list"May contain" warning
What it tells youEverything deliberately added to the productAn allergen that might turn up by accident
Required by lawYes, alwaysNo, it is voluntary
How certain is itShould be completely accurateBased on the manufacturer's own risk assessment
Where to find itThe main ingredients panel, allergens shown in boldUsually its own line near the ingredients list

Two jars of the same type of biscuit from two different manufacturers might list identical ingredients and still carry different "may contain" warnings, because each factory has assessed its own layout, its own equipment, and its own cleaning process. Neither label is necessarily wrong. They are simply describing two different production lines with two different levels of accidental risk.

Why the warning shows up so often

For a lot of shoppers, seeing "may contain" on almost everything eventually leads to a kind of fatigue, where the warning stops feeling meaningful because it is everywhere. That reaction is understandable, but it can be risky if it causes someone to stop reading labels carefully at all.

31% of people with a food allergy or intolerance say they avoid market stalls because of worries about undeclared allergens

Almost as many, 29%, avoid takeaways and delivery food for the same reason

That level of caution shows how much trust is riding on a label being both accurate and genuinely risk based, rather than a blanket disclaimer added out of caution on the manufacturer's side. The Food Standards Agency has said plainly that a precautionary label should reflect a real, assessed risk of cross contamination, not simply cover a business against every possibility.

A "may contain" label should reflect a genuine, assessed risk, not a business covering itself against every possibility.

Standardising how PAL is applied across the industry has been discussed for years, including proposals for clearer, more consistent wording so that "may contain" means roughly the same thing wherever you see it. Until that happens, the safest approach is to treat every warning as specific to that product and that factory, rather than assuming it means the same level of risk everywhere.

How to actually read a label, step by step

None of this needs to be complicated once you have a routine. Here is a simple order to work through the next time you are checking a product, whether it is for yourself or for someone else in your family.

Read the ingredients list first

This is the legally required part, and it tells you what has actually gone into the product on purpose.

Look for the allergens shown in bold

UK labels highlight all 14 major allergens within the ingredients list itself, so they should stand out even in a long list.

Take "may contain" seriously if a reaction has ever been severe

For anyone with a history of a strong reaction, a precautionary warning is usually worth treating as a real no rather than a formality.

Recheck products bought regularly

Recipes and factories change over time. A product that was safe six months ago can carry a new warning today, so it is worth a second look each time.

Scan it if there is any doubt

Apps like BiteInsight pull the ingredients and allergen information straight from a barcode in seconds, which is a quick way to double check a label before it goes in the trolley.

Frequently asked questions

Does "may contain nuts" mean the product definitely has nuts in it

No. It means there is a chance of trace contamination during manufacturing, not that nuts were used as an ingredient. If nuts were deliberately added, they would appear in the main ingredients list in bold instead.

Is precautionary allergen labelling a legal requirement

No. Declaring the 14 major allergens when they are used as an ingredient is a legal requirement. "May contain" warnings are voluntary and added at the manufacturer's discretion, based on their own risk assessment.

Why do two similar products carry different warnings

Each manufacturer assesses their own factory, equipment and cleaning process separately. Two products with identical ingredients lists can still end up with different precautionary warnings because the accidental contamination risk is different on each production line.

What should I do if a product has no allergen information at all

For prepacked food, full ingredient and allergen labelling is required by law, including on food made and packed on site under Natasha's Law. If information genuinely seems to be missing, contact the manufacturer directly, or ask staff for the full ingredients list before buying loose or freshly prepared food.

The Bottom Line

A full ingredients list tells you what is definitely in a product, while "may contain" is one manufacturer's own judgement about accidental risk. Read both, take severe allergies seriously every time, and when a label leaves you unsure, a quick scan beats a guess.
