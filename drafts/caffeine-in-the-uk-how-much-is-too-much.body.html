The UK runs on caffeine, quite literally. A coffee to start the day, tea at your desk, maybe a cola or an energy drink to push through the afternoon slump. Most of us never add it up. But caffeine is a genuinely active drug, and knowing roughly how much is in what you are drinking, and how your own body clears it, makes a real difference to how you sleep, how you feel, and how much you are actually taking in.

How much caffeine is actually safe

The Food Standards Agency's guidance, in line with the European Food Safety Authority's assessment, puts the safe upper limit for a healthy adult at 400 milligrams a day, taken as no more than 200 milligrams in any single dose. For pregnant women, the FSA halves that to 200 milligrams a day, because caffeine crosses the placenta and a developing baby cannot clear it the way an adult can.

That drops to 200mg a day during pregnancy

Four hundred milligrams sounds generous until you realise how quickly ordinary drinks add up across a normal day, especially once tea, coffee and a soft drink or two are all in the mix.

What is actually in your cup

Caffeine content depends far more on brewing method than most people expect. A filter coffee generally has more caffeine than an espresso, simply because the serving is so much bigger.

That decaf figure catches a lot of people out. If you are cutting back for a pregnancy, a sleep problem, or a health condition, decaf is a big improvement but it is not zero, and several cups can still add up.

The energy drink problem, and the new under 16s ban

Energy drinks are where caffeine intake quietly gets out of hand, especially for younger drinkers. A typical 500ml energy drink can carry well over 150 milligrams of caffeine, more than a strong filter coffee, in a drink that is marketed and priced like a soft drink rather than treated like one.

From April 2027, England will ban the sale of any drink containing more than 150 milligrams of caffeine per litre to anyone under 16. Ordinary tea, coffee and standard soft drinks such as cola are exempt, since none of them come close to that threshold at normal serving sizes. Retailers carry the legal responsibility for checking age at the till, with fines of up to 2,500 pounds for breaking the rules.

Strong evidence links high caffeine energy drinks in children and teenagers to anxiety, poor sleep, reduced concentration, and harm to learning and general wellbeing.

If there are teenagers in your household, the practical takeaway is simple. It is not the odd can of cola that is the concern, it is energy drinks specifically, because the caffeine concentration in a normal serving is so much higher than almost anything else on the shelf.

The warning label that is already on the shelf

Long before the under 16s ban, UK and EU food information rules already required a warning on high caffeine drinks. Since December 2014, any drink containing more than 150 milligrams of caffeine per litre, other than tea or coffee products that use the word tea or coffee in their name, has to carry the words "High caffeine content. Not recommended for children or pregnant or breast feeding women" in the same field of vision as the product name, along with the caffeine amount in milligrams per 100 millilitres.

That exemption for anything branded as tea or coffee is worth knowing, because it means a bottled iced coffee drink can sit above the threshold and still carry no warning at all, simply because of what it is called. The label rule was written for energy drinks and similar products, not as a complete caffeine disclosure system, so it is still worth checking the actual milligrams per 100 millilitres on the pack rather than assuming no warning means low caffeine.

Look for the warning wording itself

"High caffeine content" on a can or bottle means the product is above 150mg per litre. That is a genuine legal signal, not marketing language.

Check the per 100ml figure, not just the warning

The label has to state caffeine per 100ml. Multiply by the can or bottle size to get the real total for that serving.

Do not assume tea and coffee drinks are automatically low

Bottled coffee drinks are exempt from the warning by name alone, so a strong one can carry a serious amount of caffeine with no label cue at all.

Why the same coffee hits people so differently

If a coffee at 4pm ruins your sleep while a friend can drink one after dinner and doze off fine, that is not really about willpower or habit. It comes down to a gene called CYP1A2, which controls the enzyme that breaks caffeine down in your liver.

are fast metabolisers, clearing half a dose in around two hours

50%

are intermediate metabolisers, with moderate clearance

10%

are slow metabolisers, taking up to four times as long

A slow metaboliser can still have a meaningful amount of caffeine in their system eight hours after their last cup. That is a genuine biological difference, not a matter of discipline, and it is worth knowing which end of the range you are likely to sit on if caffeine seems to affect you more than it affects the people around you.

It also matters for anyone prone to anxiety, heart palpitations, or a jittery, wired feeling after what felt like an ordinary coffee. If that sounds familiar, you are more likely to be a slow metaboliser carrying a higher effective dose for longer, rather than simply being more sensitive in some vague sense. That is worth mentioning to a GP if palpitations or anxiety symptoms are new or getting worse, since caffeine is a simple, reversible thing to test by cutting back for a couple of weeks.

Two other things slow caffeine clearance further, worth knowing if either applies to you. Taking the combined oral contraceptive pill roughly doubles how long caffeine stays in your system, because oestrogen competes for the same liver enzyme. Pregnancy slows clearance even more, which is part of why the pregnancy guidance is set so much lower than the general adult limit.

Caffeine and sleep: when to actually stop

Caffeine has a half life of roughly five to seven hours in an average adult, meaning half of what you drank is often still active in your system that many hours later, and a meaningful trace can remain well beyond that.

Set a cutoff time, not just a cup limit

Most sleep researchers suggest stopping caffeine at least eight to ten hours before bed, which for a normal bedtime means nothing after early afternoon.

Move the cutoff earlier if you are a slow metaboliser

If caffeine reliably disrupts your sleep even from a lunchtime coffee, treat yourself as being on the slower end and cut off earlier still.

Remember hidden sources

Some soft drinks, chocolate, and pre workout supplements all add to the daily total even when coffee itself is off the table.

Swap gradually, not overnight

Cutting caffeine suddenly can cause headaches and low mood for a few days. Stepping down with half caff or decaf blends avoids the worst of it.

Common questions

Is decaf completely caffeine free?

No. A decaf coffee typically still has around 2 to 15 milligrams of caffeine per cup, far less than a regular coffee, but not zero. Several cups a day can still add up to a noticeable amount.

Is it the caffeine in energy drinks that is the concern, or something else?

Caffeine is the main concern regulators point to, since a normal serving can carry more than a strong coffee in a drink often consumed quickly and repeatedly. Some energy drinks also contain added sugar and other stimulants, which is a separate issue on top of the caffeine itself.

Why does caffeine affect some people so much more than others?

Largely genetics. Variation in the CYP1A2 gene changes how quickly your liver breaks caffeine down, so two people drinking the same coffee can clear it at very different speeds. Hormonal factors such as the contraceptive pill and pregnancy slow clearance further.

Is 400 milligrams a day actually a lot?

It is roughly four cups of filter coffee, or a similar mix of tea and coffee across a day. Most people stay well under it without trying, but it is easy to go over once energy drinks or several strong coffees are involved.

Could caffeine be behind my heart palpitations or anxiety?

It is worth ruling out, especially if you are on the slower end of the metaboliser range or take the contraceptive pill. Cutting back for a couple of weeks is a low risk way to see whether symptoms improve, though new or worsening palpitations are always worth mentioning to a GP regardless of the cause.

The Bottom Line

Caffeine is not the enemy, but it adds up faster than most people track, and it affects everyone at a different speed. Know roughly what is in your regular drinks, know which end of the metaboliser range you likely sit on, and give yourself a proper cutoff time before bed.
